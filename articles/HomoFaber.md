Regarding human intelligence,
we have not sufficiently noticed that mechanical invention are originally at its core,
that our social life revolves around the manufacture and use of artificial tools to this day,
that the inventions which mark the road of progress have also set its direction.
We hardly notice, because the changes in mankind lag behind the advances of its tooling.
Our individual habits, and even social habits,
far outlive the circumstances for which they were built,
such that an invention's full impact can only be felt as its novelty fades.
A century has passed since the birth of the steam engine, and only now are we beginning to feel the shock of its arrival.
The revolution it instigated in industry has nonetheless overturned relationships between people.

[...]

In thousands of years, when only the outlines have survived the ages, our wars and our revolutions will be a matter of indifference, if remembered at all; but the steam engine, and all the inventions that followed in its tracks, will perhaps be spoken of as we speak of bronze or stone tools; it will define an age. Could we abandon all pride, could we, in order to define our species, stick strictly to the unchanging characteristic of man and intelligence, unveiled by history and prehistory, we might not say _Homo sapiens_, but _Homo faber_. In short, _intelligence, considered in what seems to be its original approach, is the aptitude to craft artificial objects, particularly tool-making tools, and indefinitely diversify their fabrication._
